Cognitive Tutor makes a learning hypothesis testable. Its software offers feedback and practice based on a model of algebra skills, while teachers implement the curriculum. In a large randomized evaluation, high schools assigned to the package performed better in year two. The study did not isolate the contribution of the software or show that the same approach works for every subject.

In brief

Carnegie Learning’s Cognitive Tutor applies cognitive models and Bayesian knowledge tracing to guide algebra practice. In the RAND evaluation, 54 high schools and 68 middle schools were assigned to the blended curriculum package or to continue their existing algebra curriculum. The high-school effect was positive in year two, with no significant first-year effect and no significant second-year middle-school effect. The trial tested the curriculum, software and its implementation as a package. It did not isolate knowledge tracing, establish long-term retention, or explain why the second-year high-school result differed.

The case in five beats

  1. Cognitive modeling gave the developers a way to specify component algebra skills.
  2. Step-level observations inform a mastery estimate and guide practice.
  3. Teachers and students use the software as part of a blended curriculum.
  4. Schools were randomized to the package or existing curriculum.
  5. The high-school benefit appeared in year two; the mechanism behind the timing remains open.
