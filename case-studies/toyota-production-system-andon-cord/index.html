Toyota Production System / Andon Cord

About this resource · How to use it · Learning engineering connections

What is this resource?

A documented case about Toyota Production System / Andon Cord. A front-line signal summons a team leader during the work cycle. A fixed-position stop gives the leader time to respond.

How should I use it?

  1. Read the narrative and five beats, keeping the disclosures and scope limits in view.
  2. Compare the LE insight with the LENS approach. Identify the system condition that changed what people could do, then check the cited evidence before applying the lesson elsewhere.

How does this connect to LENS and learning engineering?

Andon pairs a signal with a staffed response and a shared standard. The opportunity to learn comes from investigating a defect or delay and revising work when a pattern recurs. The same signal can carry less information if people are discouraged from using it. Observe how workers recognize abnormalities, what team leaders do when called and how recurring problems change standard work. Evaluate response quality and repeated defects alongside pull counts. Ask workers whether the formal permission to raise concerns matches practice.

Pulling it does not stop the line immediately. In Toyota’s fixed-position arrangement the andon cord summons a team leader while the line moves toward a designated stop point. Workers need a standard that lets them recognize a problem, and team leaders need time and authority to respond.

At NUMMI, managers described frequent calls and problems resolved without stopping the line. Some workers interviewed by Paul Adler reported pressure to avoid calling or leaders resetting the cord without investigating. That difference is part of the case. When adapting andon, measure whether workers can identify and raise problems, whether leaders respond, and whether recurring defects lead to improvements in the work.

In brief

Under Toyota’s fixed-position stop system, a worker can call for help when work departs from the standard. The line continues moving while a team leader responds. The leader can resolve the issue within the cycle or let the affected segment stop at the designated position. In Paul Adler’s NUMMI field research, managers described calls that resolved problems without stopping the line. Some workers described pressure to avoid pulls or leaders resetting the cord without investigating. The accounts show how staffing, authority and local response practice affect what the cord enables. The contribution of each part to production quality is not separately measured.

The case in five beats

  1. Standardized work helps workers recognize an abnormality
  2. The worker signals for help while the line keeps moving
  3. A team leader responds within the work cycle
  4. NUMMI workers and managers described different response practices
  5. Measure how signals change response, repeat defects and standards

Competing readings

Compare what the sources agree on, where their findings differ, and how each interpretation would change what you test or design.

  • A readily available call mechanism can lower the cost of speaking up. A staffed response and permission to use it may matter more than the cord itself.
  • NUMMI management described prompt help and frequent calls resolved without stopping. Some workers interviewed by Adler described pressure to avoid pulling or reset without investigating.
  • A high pull count could indicate effective reporting or repeated unresolved problems. Check a sample of responses and outcomes.

What this case does not show

The evidence concerns a local production control and does not isolate its effect on quality or establish that it can surface design and organization-level hazards.
