Case Studies

Case Studies: What happened, and what can we learn?

About this resource · How to use it · Learning engineering connections

What is this resource?

A source-linked collection of documented cases, grouped by domain to compare failures, interventions and capability designs. Each case preserves its evidence qualifications and scope limits.

How should I use it?

  1. Choose a domain and read two cases. Compare the system conditions, actions and outcomes before drawing a lesson.
  2. Follow the sources and disclosures, then propose a new context where the explanation might fail. The linked casebook provides the wider reading edition.

How does this connect to LENS and learning engineering?

Contrasting cases support conditional judgment about human and system performance. LENS asks which capability, information, authority or support changed what people could do; a historical association alone does not establish that a proposed intervention will work elsewhere.

Compare what people could do, what the system made possible, and what mattered to the people affected. Some cases document learning engineering. Others show where it could directly address a failure, or help you distinguish a capability problem from a technical or governance problem.

Keep all three in view.

Human
Who gained or lost agency, opportunity or well-being? Whose experience is missing from the record?
Science
What evidence supports the proposed learning explanation, and what other causes could explain the outcome?
System
Which tools, responsibilities, interfaces or conditions enabled the result or prevented recovery?

How to read the cards: “What failed” and “What worked” describe the reported outcome in the case, not a judgment of the people involved. Paired cases invite comparison; they do not establish that one factor caused an outcome. Competency tags identify the LENS practices exercised, while topic domains identify the setting. Why LENS uses a casebook

The casebook puts the argument of Capability Matters into practice. What people can accomplish depends on what they know and can do, and on what the system makes possible. The cases ask where a gap came from, what could change it, and what evidence would justify acting. Ask who benefits, who carries the burden, and who has the authority to act or contest a decision.

I-PASS shows how resident practice, faculty feedback, and a structured handoff work together. In the INL turbine-control case, operators used a simulator to expose interface problems before formal validation. Failure cases let readers design and test a response to a documented gap while keeping the proposed response distinct from what happened.

Rwanda RapidSMS shows why sending a referral and completing it require different evidence. The Latin American Learning Analytics project shows how local inquiry shaped advising tools and pilots. Navy Surface Warfare Readiness Reform asks how to tell whether assessed skills persist and transfer to supervised work at sea.

The LENS approach moves from Understand through Map, Design, Build, Instrument, Experiment, and Evaluate, then returns to Understand. Refine throughout. Pick a case, define the work, map what people and systems must provide, and specify a result that would make you revise the design.
