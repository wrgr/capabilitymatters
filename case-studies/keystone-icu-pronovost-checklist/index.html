Keystone ICU / Pronovost Checklist

About this resource · How to use it · Learning engineering connections

What is this resource?

A documented case about Keystone ICU / Pronovost Checklist. Across 103 Michigan ICUs, the median reported infection rate fell from 2.7 to 0 per 1,000 catheter-days at three months. Low rates persisted in follow-up.

How should I use it?

  1. Read the narrative and five beats, keeping the disclosures and scope limits in view.
  2. Compare the LE insight with the LENS approach. Identify the system condition that changed what people could do, then check the cited evidence before applying the lesson elsewhere.

How does this connect to LENS and learning engineering?

Michigan’s Keystone program gave nurses permission to stop a non-emergency central-line insertion when a sterile step was missed. That authority came with equipment, education, a shared checklist and monthly infection feedback. The median reported infection rate reached zero at three months across the participating ICUs. The cohort evaluated the package as a whole, leaving open how much any component contributed. England’s experience with a related program shows why a new site needs its own evidence.

In brief

The Michigan Keystone ICU program combined established central-line infection prevention practices with clinician education, a dedicated line cart, daily catheter review and monthly infection-rate feedback. Nurses could stop non-emergency insertions when steps were missed. In a prospective quality-improvement cohort across 103 ICUs, the median reported infection rate fell to zero at three months. The modeled incidence rate at 16–18 months was 66% below baseline. The study evaluated the package as implemented. Nurse stop authority is a plausible mechanism, alongside education, supplies, feedback and the collaborative.

The case in five beats

  1. The intervention made established sterile procedures easier to perform reliably
  2. Nurses gained explicit authority to stop a non-emergency insertion
  3. Education, equipment and feedback supported the shared procedure
  4. The reported ICU median reached zero, with low rates sustained at follow-up
  5. England’s comparison illustrates why transfer and causal attribution need separate evidence

LENS approach

Observe whether a clinician notices a missing sterile step and whether colleagues can resolve the interruption safely. A simulation or process audit can measure that mechanism. Infection rates address a different claim. Specify evidence for both before transferring the bundle to a new unit.

Competing readings

Compare what the sources agree on, where their findings differ, and how each interpretation would change what you test or design.

  • Dixon-Woods et al. identify complementary mechanisms including peer norms and the collaborative’s feedback infrastructure. The cohort does not isolate the contribution of nurse stop authority.
  • England’s Matching Michigan program reported a parallel decline in pre-ICU infections, consistent with a wider secular trend.

What this case does not show

The 2006 cohort measured reported infections, not directly observed lives saved or savings, and did not randomize program components.
