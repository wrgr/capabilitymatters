I-PASS Handoff Bundle

About this resource · How to use it · Learning engineering connections

What is this resource?

A documented case about I-PASS Handoff Bundle. After implementation at nine pediatric residency programs, medical errors declined by 23% and preventable adverse events by 30% relative to baseline.

How should I use it?

  1. Read the narrative and five beats, keeping the disclosures and scope limits in view.
  2. Compare the LE insight with the LENS approach. Identify the system condition that changed what people could do, then check the cited evidence before applying the lesson elsewhere.

How does this connect to LENS and learning engineering?

A reliable handoff depends on what both clinicians can do and on whether the workflow gives them a shared structure and time to check understanding. Understand and map what the incoming clinician needs to know and do. Design and build a handoff template and practice routine with faculty feedback. Instrument handoff completeness, receiver synthesis, workload, and patient harm separately. Experiment with representative handoffs, evaluate missed information and recovery, and refine the support before adapting it to another service.

At shift change, the incoming resident must understand the patient’s condition, what to do next, and what to watch for. I-PASS gives the two clinicians a shared structure for that work: illness severity, patient summary, action list, situation awareness and contingency planning, and synthesis by the receiver. Resident practice and faculty feedback helped teams put that structure into use. Compare with Keystone ICU to see how two interventions combine learning with changes to everyday clinical work.

In brief

I-PASS combined a structured oral and written handoff with resident education, faculty observation and feedback, and support for sustaining the practice. The receiving clinician synthesized the handoff to check their understanding. Across 10,740 admissions, the medical error rate fell from 24.5 to 18.8 per 100 admissions. Written and oral handoffs improved without a significant change in handoff duration or observed resident workflow. The before-and-after study evaluated the whole bundle.

The case in five beats

  1. Shift change transfers responsibility as well as information.
  2. The bundle combines a handoff structure with training and faculty reinforcement.
  3. Receiver synthesis makes misunderstandings visible while the outgoing clinician is present.
  4. Researchers observed handoff quality, workflow, and patient safety outcomes.
  5. Medical error reductions were statistically significant at six of nine sites.

What this case does not show

The before-and-after design cannot isolate the bundle’s effect from other changes over time or separate the effects of training, the mnemonic, and the other components. Results varied across sites.
