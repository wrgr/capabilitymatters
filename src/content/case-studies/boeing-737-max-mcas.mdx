The 737 MAX depended on crews recognizing and responding to uncommanded trim under pressure. Boeing treated avoiding simulator differences training as a design objective, and original manuals and training did not explain MCAS. In both accidents an erroneous angle-of-attack signal could trigger repeated nose-down trim. Indonesia’s investigators identified nine factors in the Lion Air crash. Ethiopia’s investigators, the NTSB and France’s BEA agreed on MCAS’s contribution to Ethiopian 302, but the NTSB disputed the electrical explanation for the sensor fault and both outside agencies called for fuller treatment of crew performance.

The FAA’s response changed what the aircraft would do as well as what crews were told and trained to do. A realistic simulator assessment can test the combined response, including whether crews can diagnose and act amid multiple alerts within the available time. The investigations cannot establish the effect simulator training alone would have had on either flight.
